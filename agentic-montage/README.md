# Agentic Montage

A real-time simulation of the agent-based media-montage system: the agent reads
a corpus of declassified documents and speaks about what it sees, in subtitles,
over a montage that keeps moving, while a locator shows where on Earth the page
on screen is about.

Live at `/sites/agentic-montage/`.

## The corpus

Pages from the USA Government UAP release, chosen across handwritten reports,
teletype memoranda, newspaper clippings, photostat checklists, incident summary
sheets, NASA crew transcripts, FBI interviews and modern mission reports, so the
montage keeps changing texture. They run from a 1945 "foo fighter" over the
Franco-German border to the Western U.S. orb events of 2026, and from Perth to
Kodiak and out to lunar orbit.

- `corpus.js` is the manifest — the only file you edit to change the piece.
- `media/01.jpg` … are the pages.
- `world.js` is the coastline the locator draws. It is data, not settings.
- `index.html` is markup only; `style.css` has the five layers, `index.js` the
  clock, the sequencer and the locator.
- Each entry keeps `ref`, the archival reference for the page, `summary`, the
  pipeline's summary of it, verbatim, and `place`, where on Earth it is about.

Every subtitle in `lines` is an excerpt cut verbatim out of that page's summary,
so the agent only ever speaks its own description of what is on screen. The
manifest is generated by locating each excerpt in a normalised copy of the
summary and slicing it out of the original, which means an excerpt that is not
really in the summary is reported rather than silently written.

Excerpts are cut mid-sentence, because that is where the sentence they came from
started. Reading them as sentences is a presentation job and stays in
`index.js`: it raises the first letter and closes the line off with a full stop
unless the excerpt already ends in one. `corpus.js` keeps the verbatim slice.

The size of the corpus is never shown on screen, so entries can be added or
removed without the page announcing how much there is.

## What plays next

Not the array order. The corpus is dealt into a bag, shuffled, and drawn from
one page at a time; when the bag empties it is re-cut. That gives three things
at once:

- every page shows once before any page shows twice,
- the montage opens somewhere different every time it is loaded, so a visitor
  who comes back does not get the same page and the same sentence,
- no page follows itself across the seam where one pass ends and the next
  begins, because the re-cut knows what was last on screen.

After the shuffle, a second pass walks the bag and swaps any page that landed
within `MIN_APART` degrees of the one before it further down the order, so the
reticle keeps crossing the world instead of lingering in one region. Over two
thousand passes of the current corpus that takes neighbours closer than 25° from
21.8% of hops down to 0.7%, with no page ever repeating back to back and every
one of the sixty opening the piece at some point. The mean hop is 109°.

The bag is re-cut about every ten minutes and the spread pass is a few hundred
comparisons, so none of this shows up in a frame budget.

Only the page on screen and the one drawn next are ever fetched, so this costs
no more bandwidth than playing in order did.

## The locator

Pinned under the top rule: a plate-carrée world map, a reticle on the current
page's coordinates, and place, region, decimal position and date beside it. When
the document changes the reticle flies to the new position and a hairline traces
the hop it just made, so a viewer who looks up mid-piece can see where the
montage has got to.

Under the map itself a caption names the document family the page on screen
belongs to — `FBI HQ FILE`, `NASA APOLLO`, `USG UAP REPORTS`, … — so a viewer
can tell at a glance whether the agent is reading a 1947 memorandum or a 2023
mission report.

The date is taken off the end of the same `id` the footer prints, rather than
stored twice, so the locator and the bar can never disagree about which page is
on screen. Two OCR-read dates are known to be wrong; a tiny curated table in
`index.js`, keyed by page, corrects them at load — the summaries stay verbatim
and the two readouts still can't disagree. All four lines are the same white:
the hierarchy is size and tracking, which survives a pale scan drifting
underneath better than a grey would.

It is anchored to the top of the frame rather than stacked above the subtitle,
so it holds still while the subtitle grows and shrinks underneath it.

`place` is `[name, region, lat, lon]` and comes from the page itself — the town
in the sighting, the sea the mission flew over, the embassy the cable left from.
Every page in the current corpus carries one: a transcript or report whose
subject has no location — a space mission, a redacted test range — is pinned to
where it originated, Houston for the NASA crew debriefings, the base a mission
report flew from, the facility the redacted FBI and USPER reports point to. An
entry with no `place` would read UNLOCATED rather than pointing somewhere
invented, and the locator never guesses.

The outline is Natural Earth 1:110m land (public domain), simplified to one
11 kB path, clipped to 84°N–58°S. It is drawn once and never redrawn; only the
reticle moves, on a transform, so the map costs nothing per frame.

## Preparing images

From the release scans:

1. Greyscale.
2. Invert if the mean is below 100 — photostats are white type on black, and
   inverting puts every page at the same density behind the subtitles.
3. Trim the scanner surround: threshold at the 5th–90th percentile midpoint,
   keep rows and columns where more than 15% of pixels are above it.
4. Autocontrast, cutoff 0.5.
5. Fit inside 1100 × 1320 and save as progressive JPEG, quality 80.

That is about 180 kB a page. Only the page on screen and the one after it are
ever fetched, so the montage costs one image per twenty seconds however long the
corpus grows.

On a landscape screen the plate is opened out past the frame (`inset: -26%`) so
a portrait page fills the width instead of letterboxing to half of it, and the
drift crops it; portrait screens show the page whole.

## Shots

A page does not sit still for the twenty seconds its summary takes to say. Every
subtitle line gets its own shot: the same page, framed somewhere else and at a
different distance, dissolved into over the line before it. Three or four
sentences means three or four shots, so the montage moves at the rate the agent
talks.

The page arriving takes the long dissolve, 2.4 s. The shots inside a document
take 800 ms, because it is the same paper, not a new one.

This costs no bandwidth at all — a shot is a second framing of an image the
browser has already decoded, so the piece still fetches one image per document.
It costs one extra plate for the length of a dissolve, which is why the dissolve
inside a document is short. Framings are seeded by slot and shot, so a page is
always read the same way round.

`prefers-reduced-motion` turns shots off: the page arrives once and drifts, as
it did before.

## Changing the corpus

1. Put the pages in `media/`.
2. Point `src` at them in `corpus.js`.
3. Give each one a `place`, taken from the page, not guessed.
4. Write `lines` — these are the subtitles, and their combined length decides how
   long each page stays on screen. Three lines is roughly twenty seconds.
5. Optionally add `audio: "media/01.mp3"` for a voice-over. If any entry has
   audio the page opens with a click-to-begin gate, because browsers block
   autoplay. Subtitle timing stays driven by `lines`, not by the audio file.

An entry with `src: null` falls back to a procedurally generated scan, seeded by
its slot in the list, so the loop is identical on every run. A `src` that 404s
falls back the same way rather than showing a blank frame.

## What it costs to run

The piece is meant to be left on a projector overnight, so nothing in it grows
without bound and nothing is recomputed that has not changed:

- One `requestAnimationFrame` loop drives everything, and it does no work at all
  while the tab is hidden.
- The state columns are a fixed ring of spans, sized to the column on load and
  on resize. Each tick recycles the oldest line to the bottom and rewrites it —
  a move, not an allocation, so the log scrolls without the DOM ever growing.
- The timecodes are written only when their digits change.
- Subtitle words get their class once, as they are revealed, rather than every
  frame.
- The vignette, scrim and scan lines are promoted to their own layers and
  contained, so a drifting plate underneath never repaints them. The scan lines
  are plain low-opacity rules; blending them into the whole frame cost more than
  it was worth.
- The pages are already greyscale, so only the generated stand-ins pay for a
  `grayscale()` filter.
- Plates are dropped two-deep and their animations cancelled with the node, and
  a plate on its way out is removed as soon as its dissolve is over rather than
  on a fixed timer, so a shot change never leaves a third plate animating behind
  the frame.

## Controls

- `space` pause / resume — everything runs off one clock, so it stops exactly
- `c` hide the chrome (columns, timecodes, footer), leaving montage, locator
  and subtitle
- `f` fullscreen
- on touch, tap anywhere to pause

The cursor hides after three seconds. For a conference: load the page, `f`, then
`c` if you want the bare projection. The montage pauses by itself when the
window is hidden and resumes without jumping, so a machine left on the page
overnight will not have drifted.
